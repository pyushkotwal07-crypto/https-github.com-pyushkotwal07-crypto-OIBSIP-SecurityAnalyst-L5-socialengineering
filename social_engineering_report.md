# Task 5: Social Engineering

## 1. Introduction
Social engineering is a cybersecurity technique in which an attacker manipulates people into revealing confidential information, approving an unsafe action, or giving access to systems. Instead of relying only on technical vulnerabilities, these attacks exploit trust, urgency, curiosity, fear, or helpfulness.

## 2. Objectives
- Understand the meaning and common forms of social engineering.
- Recognize warning signs in messages, calls, websites, and in-person requests.
- Learn practical ways to prevent and report social engineering incidents.

## 3. Common Types of Social Engineering

### 3.1 Phishing
Attackers send deceptive emails or messages that appear to come from a trusted organization. They may try to make a recipient open a malicious attachment, visit a fake login page, or disclose sensitive information.

**Warning signs:** unexpected requests, suspicious links, mismatched sender addresses, unusual attachments, spelling errors, or pressure to act quickly.

**Prevention:** verify the sender independently, inspect links before opening them, avoid unexpected attachments, and report suspicious messages.

### 3.2 Spear Phishing
This is a targeted form of phishing tailored to a particular person or organization. Attackers may use publicly available details to make a message seem genuine.

**Prevention:** verify unusual requests through a separate trusted channel and avoid sharing unnecessary personal or organizational information publicly.

### 3.3 Vishing and Smishing
- **Vishing:** deceptive voice calls, often pretending to be a bank, support agent, or official.
- **Smishing:** deceptive SMS or text messages containing fraudulent requests or links.

**Prevention:** do not disclose passwords, PINs, or one-time passcodes to callers or message senders. Contact the organization using a phone number or website you independently know is genuine.

### 3.4 Pretexting
An attacker invents a believable story or identity to persuade someone to provide information or perform an action—for example, pretending to be an employee who needs account access.

**Prevention:** follow identity-verification procedures and never bypass approval processes just because a request sounds urgent.

### 3.5 Baiting
An attacker offers something appealing, such as a free download or an unknown storage device, to encourage unsafe behavior.

**Prevention:** avoid unknown USB devices and untrusted downloads; use approved software sources and security tools.

### 3.6 Tailgating
An unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted area without using their own access credentials.

**Prevention:** do not hold secure doors open for unknown people; follow visitor and access-control policies and politely direct visitors to reception.

### 3.7 Business Email Compromise (BEC)
An attacker impersonates an executive, colleague, or supplier to request a payment, change bank details, or obtain sensitive data.

**Prevention:** verify payment changes and unusual financial requests through a known, separate communication channel. Require documented approval for transfers.

## 4. Typical Attack Process
1. **Research:** the attacker gathers information about a target or organization.
2. **Contact:** the attacker sends a message, makes a call, or approaches the target.
3. **Manipulation:** urgency, authority, fear, curiosity, or trust is used to influence the target.
4. **Requested action:** the target may disclose information, click a link, approve a request, or allow access.
5. **Misuse:** the attacker may attempt account takeover, fraud, data theft, or unauthorized access.

This is a general awareness model, not a guide for carrying out an attack.

## 5. Warning Signs
- A demand for immediate action or secrecy.
- Requests for passwords, PINs, recovery codes, or one-time passcodes.
- Unexpected links, attachments, or login prompts.
- A sender address or web domain that does not match the claimed organization.
- Requests to change payment details or bypass normal procedures.
- Offers that seem unusually generous or too good to be true.
- A caller who refuses independent verification.

## 6. Prevention and Best Practices
- Use multi-factor authentication (MFA), preferably phishing-resistant MFA where available.
- Use unique passwords and a reputable password manager.
- Verify sensitive requests through a separate, trusted channel.
- Keep operating systems, browsers, and security software updated.
- Use email filtering and endpoint protection where available.
- Limit personal information shared publicly.
- Complete regular cybersecurity-awareness training.
- Follow least-privilege access and approval procedures.
- Report suspicious messages or incidents promptly to the appropriate IT/security team.
- If credentials may have been exposed, change them using the official site, revoke active sessions where possible, and notify the responsible security team.

## 7. What to Do if You Suspect an Attack
1. Do not click further links, reply, or provide additional information.
2. Report the message or incident using the organization's approved process.
3. If you entered credentials, change the password from the official website and notify the IT/security team.
4. If you approved a payment or shared financial information, contact the relevant bank or payment provider promptly.
5. Preserve the suspicious message and relevant details for the security team; do not forward it widely.

## 8. Comparison Table

| Technique | Typical channel | Main warning sign | Key defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phishing | Email or web page | Unexpected link or attachment | Verify sender and URL |
| Spear phishing | Targeted email/message | Personalized but unusual request | Independent verification |
| Vishing | Phone call | Pressure to disclose codes or details | Call back using a trusted number |
| Smishing | SMS/text | Unexpected link or urgent alert | Use official apps/sites |
| Pretexting | Call, email, or in person | Unverified identity or story | Confirm identity and authorization |
| Baiting | Download or physical media | Unknown device or tempting offer | Avoid untrusted media and downloads |
| Tailgating | Physical access | Someone requests entry without credentials | Follow access-control rules |
| BEC | Business email | Payment or bank-detail change | Confirm using a separate channel |

## 9. Conclusion
Social engineering remains a major cybersecurity risk because it targets human decision-making as well as technology. Awareness, independent verification, strong authentication, clear approval processes, and prompt reporting can significantly reduce the likelihood and impact of these attacks.

## 10. Learning Outcomes
After completing this task, a learner should be able to:
- Define social engineering and identify common techniques.
- Recognize suspicious communication and manipulation tactics.
- Apply safe verification and reporting practices.
- Explain how MFA, training, and organizational procedures reduce risk.
